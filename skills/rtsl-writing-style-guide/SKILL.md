# Resolve to Save Lives (RTSL) writing style guide

## Pre-flight checklist

Run every document through this 60-second check before finalizing.

1. **Identify the tier.** Is this for the public (Tier 1), partners (Tier 2) or scientists (Tier 3)?
2. **The "success" test.** Did I highlight our partners' work and our partnership more than RTSL's solo success?
3. **The 3 C's.** Is it coherent, clear and concise? (Check for "throat clearing" like *the fact that* or *very*.)
4. **Grammar quick-check.**
   - Did I remove all Oxford commas?
   - Is it in active voice? (e.g., "We supported" vs. "Support was provided by.")
   - Are headers in sentence case?
   - Did I use "because" instead of "since" for causation?
   - Did I use "nearly" for numbers and "almost" for concepts?
5. **Voice check.** Do I sound like a trusted family doctor (humble, direct, expert) or a corporate textbook (pretentious, vague)?

## 1. The core philosophy: the humble physician

Our overarching voice is that of a trusted family doctor. We are experts who are humble, accessible and focused on the well-being of the person in front of us.

- **Coherence.** We don't leave out vital steps; the logic must be sound.
- **Clarity.** We avoid jargon that alienates partners.
- **Conciseness.** We value our partners' time. Every word must serve a purpose.
- **Partnership-first.** We highlight the work of our partners and the strength of the collective effort, rather than speaking only of RTSL successes.
- **Scale-first.** Always contextualize individual achievements within global scale. When a country passes a policy or a clinic adopts a program, show what it means for the wider world.

## 2. Technicality tiers (the tone scale)

Before writing, identify the audience to determine the correct sub-voice:

- **Tier 1 (public/social).** Language for lay audiences. Use "heart health" and "salt." No acronyms.
- **Tier 2 (partners/ministers).** Professional plain language. Use "CVH" and "7-1-7 approach." Focus on "support" and "accompany."
- **Tier 3 (scientific/technical).** Technical terms allowed (e.g., TFA, ACE inhibitors, protocols).

### Tones

Within each tier, adapt your tone to the situation:

- **Inspiring and motivating.** When selling RTSL to donors or decision-makers. More urgent, optimistic and confident. Less technical. Used in proposals, donor reports, success stories.
- **Affirming and recognizing.** When updating, praising or thanking partners and donors. More collaborative, diplomatic and celebratory. Used in newsletters, social media, progress reports.
- **Explaining and supporting.** When sharing technical expertise with partners. More technical and action-oriented. Less directive. Used in technical documents, factsheets, toolkits, presentations.

## 3. Style foundation and accessibility

- **Primary reference.** RTSL style is based on the AP Stylebook, with exceptions noted here.
- **Oxford comma.** Never use the Oxford comma (e.g., "apples, oranges and grapes").
- **Spelling.** Use American English (except for proper names or documents for audiences in countries using British English).
- **Voice.** Avoid passive voice. Use active, direct language.
- **Sentence length.** Aim for an average of 15 words per sentence. One idea per sentence.
- **The 3 S's.** Always in this order: Simplicity, Speed and Scale.

## 4. Branding and organizational identity

- **The organization.** Use "Resolve to Save Lives" on first mention; subsequently "RTSL" (never "Resolve").
- **Our priorities.** Use "Epidemic Prevention," "Blood Pressure Control," "Healthier Foods and Environment" and "Primary Health care." Do not abbreviate initiative titles.
- **Our role.** We "support" or "accompany" countries. We do not "help" (avoids top-down hierarchy). It is fine, however, to say an intervention "helps."
- **Health must be present.** When describing who we are and what we do, always mention health explicitly. We "accelerate action against the world's deadliest health threats to save millions of lives" — not simply "accelerate action to save millions of lives."

## 5. Word choice and the "humble physician" filter

### Critical distinctions

| Use this | Instead of this | Context/reason |
|---|---|---|
| While | Although | "While" refers only to time. Use "although" for caveats and concessions. (Note: do not replace "although" with "while" — this is a common error.) |
| Because | Since / As | "Because" establishes clear causation. |
| Almost | Nearly | "Almost" for concepts; "nearly" for numbers. |
| More than | Over | "More than" for numbers; "over" for physical location. |
| Such as | Like | "Such as" for lists; "like" for comparisons. |
| Fewer | Less | "Fewer" for countable items (people/dollars). |
| Approximately | Around | Use for estimations or quantities. |
| Use | Utilize | A humble physician simply "uses" tools. |
| Past | Last | "Past" for recent time; "last" for a final phase. |

### Words and phrases to avoid

- **"Health issues," "health problems," "diseases" or "conditions."** Use "health threats" instead.
- **"Diet."** Use "nutrition" or "food" instead. Diet has negative connotations.
- **"Prove."** Evidence *shows* work; it doesn't *prove* it.
- **"Commendable" or "incredible."** Avoid; sounds patronizing or hyperbolic.
- **"Hopefully."** Use "hope" instead.
- **"We know."** Sounds preachy. Use "studies show" or "evidence shows."
- **"Ensure."** Avoid unless listing the specific actions that guarantee the outcome.
- **Clichés.** Avoid "build back better," "viruses know no borders" or "leave no one behind."
- **Filler.** Avoid "the fact that," "very," "key" or "little/bit."

### Person-first language

- **"People" or "residents."** Avoid "citizens" or "persons."
- **Condition-first.** Say "people living with hypertension" rather than "hypertensives."
- **Outbreaks.** Say "people affected by the outbreak" rather than "victims."

## 6. Formatting, graphics and signposting

### Digital-first formatting

- **Signposting.** Bold the most important phrase in a paragraph so skimmers can get the gist instantly.
- **The paragraph sandwich.** Start and end paragraphs with short, punchy sentences.
- **Bullets.** Use parallel construction. Keep bullets to 2 lines maximum.
- **Links.** Use descriptive text (e.g., "[Read the 7-1-7 Report](link)") rather than "Click here."

### Humble graphics

- **Conclusions as titles.** Chart titles should state the finding (e.g., "Salt intake is rising") rather than the data source ("Sodium levels 2020-2024").
- **Simplicity.** Eliminate "chart junk" like 3D bars, heavy grid lines or decorative icons.

### Punctuation and dates

- **Headers.** Use sentence case (e.g., "Headers like this").
- **Em dashes.** Use with no spaces—like this.
- **Quotation marks.** Punctuation goes inside the marks.
- **Dates.** Use Jan. 17 or Jan. 17, 2017. No ordinals (1st, 2nd).
- **Currency.** Use US dollars. No need to include "US" or "USD."

## 7. Priority-specific terms

### The 7-1-7 target

- **Formatting.** Always 7-1-7 (with dashes).
- **Word choice.** Use "target" (not goal) and "approach" (not framework).

### Blood Pressure Control and Healthier Foods and Environment

- **Nutrition.** "Salt" (not sodium), "trans fat" (no hyphen), "labeling" (one 'l').
- **Terminology.** "Cardiovascular disease" (singular).
